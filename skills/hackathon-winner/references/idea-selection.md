# Idea selection method

Use this reference when generating, comparing, rejecting, or evolving hackathon concepts.

## Begin with evidence, not an empty prompt

Build an inspiration set from:

- the user's saved idea bank and bookmarks;
- recent product launches and technical demos;
- finalists, winners, and failed submissions from adjacent hackathons;
- limitations that disappeared recently because a model, protocol, device, or SDK became faster, cheaper, or newly capable;
- older or nostalgic interfaces that can be recombined with a new capability;
- the team's professional, cultural, design, research, or community knowledge.

Look for a tangential combination rather than a clone. A strong synthesis may combine inspirations from unrelated fields or different time periods. Record the source mechanics so credit and differentiation remain explicit.

Use generic AI suggestions as a negative proxy: if the same prompt predictably produces the concept, expect many teams to build it. Keep it only if the team has an unusual mechanism, interface, dataset, distribution advantage, or domain execution.

## Make the organizer technology the hero

Perform the removal test literally:

> Could the product deliver its central experience or guarantee without this technology?

If yes, the integration is ornamental. Redesign the central interaction until removing the sponsor technology breaks the product. Do not compensate by adding more sponsors. A single deeply necessary integration is stronger than five shallow ones.

Examples of load-bearing roles include:

- a fast generative model creating the visible world and assets during play;
- speech-to-text and text-to-speech forming the actual language-learning interaction;
- a privacy protocol making the financial relationship unlinkable;
- a proof system allowing an untrusted computation to settle;
- confidential compute making private policy evaluation verifiable;
- account abstraction enforcing an agent's spending limits.

A logo, wallet connect, optional NFT, generic chat panel, or post-hoc transaction is not load-bearing.

## Optimize for a judge's attention window

Assume the judge gives the project one minute before deciding whether to care.

Reject or simplify an idea when:

- the problem needs more than 30 seconds to explain;
- the pitch requires several protocol acronyms;
- the important benefit appears only after days of use;
- the demo is mostly configuration, dashboards, or invisible background work;
- the visual result is disconnected from the core technical claim.

Prefer transformations and adversarial proofs: text becomes a comic, a repository becomes a city, speech changes a live character, a forbidden transaction visibly fails, a physical scan releases escrow, or a corrupted proof is rejected.

## Test earliness

Ask:

- What became possible or practical in the last few weeks or months?
- Is the obvious version already crowded?
- Does this push a new capability to an edge that competitors have not demonstrated?
- Is the project merely automating a known workflow?
- Can an older interaction become newly viable because the technology changed?

Recent technical progress decays quickly as an advantage. Verify current products and submissions before calling an idea new.

## Use domain advantage honestly

Inventory what the team can bring before the timer starts:

- strong design taste;
- deep knowledge of a professional workflow;
- protocol, security, hardware, game, data, or AI experience;
- access to users, a dataset, equipment, or a community;
- cultural and language context;
- a reusable component or deployment pattern.

Favor ideas where this advantage changes execution quality. Do not choose a dependency such as custom hardware merely because it looks impressive if nobody can debug it under time pressure.

## Generate a bounded candidate set

Produce three to five meaningfully different concepts. For each, write:

1. one-line pitch;
2. one target user;
3. one painful need or strong desire;
4. the indispensable organizer capability;
5. the one-minute demo;
6. the team's domain advantage;
7. what makes it early;
8. the likely failure mode.

Avoid lists of twenty shallow ideas. Depth of rejection is more useful than volume.

## Score, then use judgment

Score each candidate from 0–5 on:

- organizer-tech depth;
- theme fit;
- one-line clarity;
- demo spectacle and verifiability;
- novelty/earliness;
- user value;
- domain advantage;
- deadline feasibility.

Treat any score below 3 for organizer-tech depth, theme fit, clarity, or demo as a veto unless the concept is redesigned. Totals help comparison but do not override a fatal weakness.

## Preserve rejected ideas

Record why an idea was killed: late/crowded, workflow-only, weak sponsor necessity, poor demo surface, missing domain knowledge, excessive scope, or dependence on taste the team does not possess. Rejected concepts may become viable when the technology or team changes.
